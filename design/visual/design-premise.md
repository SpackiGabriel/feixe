# Feixe — Visual Design Premise

> Creative premise for visual exploration, moodboarding, and future art-direction decisions.  
> This document defines intent, not a final design system.

## 1. Core Premise

Feixe takes place inside a mysterious retro-industrial scientific facility inspired by Soviet research environments of the 1960s and 1970s.

The player enters this facility with little understanding of where they are, what happened there, or what the machines around them were originally built to do.

At first, the environment appears functional and rational: laboratories, test chambers, optical equipment, control panels, heavy infrastructure, and machines designed for an unknown scientific purpose.

The player learns to operate parts of this machinery through puzzles involving lasers, mirrors, receivers, filters, doors, switches, and eventually portals.

However, solving the puzzles should gradually create a second question:

**What am I actually operating?**

The facility should feel as though the puzzle devices are only the accessible surface of a much larger system whose real purpose remains hidden.

---

## 2. Creative Identity

The intended aesthetic is **Soviet retro-industrial science fiction**, rather than clean contemporary science fiction.

The world should feel:

- industrial;
- institutional;
- scientific;
- analog;
- heavy;
- utilitarian;
- slightly obsolete;
- mysterious;
- increasingly uncanny.

The technology does not need to be historically accurate to the Soviet Union.

Instead, the goal is a believable alternate technological history: **what an extremely advanced optical research facility might look like if it had been designed using the industrial language of the 1960s–70s.**

The extraordinary technology and the mundane machinery surrounding it should intentionally contrast.

A laser system may perform something almost impossible while being connected to thick cables, mechanical switches, analog gauges, painted steel enclosures, and equipment that looks decades old.

---

## 3. The Facility Is Larger Than the Puzzle

Puzzle devices should not make the environment feel like a collection of game mechanics placed inside rooms.

They should appear to belong to an existing scientific installation.

The player interacts with only a small portion of a much larger machine.

Environmental elements may imply systems beyond the player's control:

- cables disappearing into walls;
- conduits crossing rooms;
- inaccessible machinery behind glass;
- ventilation and cooling infrastructure;
- numbered equipment modules;
- observation rooms;
- technical markings;
- warning labels;
- maintenance panels;
- pipes with unclear destinations;
- inactive control stations;
- large machines whose purpose is never explained.

Some infrastructure may have no gameplay function at all.

Its purpose is to imply that the facility existed before the player arrived and was built for something other than presenting puzzles.

---

## 4. Environmental Storytelling

The narrative should initially remain implicit.

The environment itself should create questions before the game provides answers.

Potential storytelling elements include:

- equipment identification numbers;
- Soviet-inspired technical typography;
- faded institutional signage;
- diagrams and schematics;
- handwritten annotations;
- damaged or modified equipment;
- abandoned workspaces;
- maintenance notes;
- experimental records;
- unexplained symbols;
- inaccessible observation areas;
- traces of previous operators;
- recurring numbers, names, or project identifiers;
- optional fragments and easter eggs.

These elements should suggest history without requiring the player to stop solving puzzles and read large amounts of exposition.

Dialogue or explicit narrative systems may be introduced later if useful, but the visual direction should not depend on them.

---

## 5. Controlled Color

The environment itself should remain predominantly neutral and desaturated.

Possible environmental colors include:

- concrete gray;
- aged off-white;
- dark painted steel;
- muted industrial green;
- faded beige;
- black;
- oxidized or worn metal.

Strong saturated color should be scarce.

This allows the laser system to dominate the visual hierarchy.

Red, green, blue, yellow, magenta, cyan, and white have mechanical meaning within the puzzle system and should therefore remain visually protected.

A red beam should not compete with arbitrary red decorative lights scattered throughout the room.

Non-laser devices such as switches, portals, structural machinery, and environmental props should therefore derive their identity primarily from:

- silhouette;
- geometry;
- iconography;
- motion;
- material;
- placement;
- neutral light;

rather than borrowing colors reserved for the laser language.

---

## 6. Light as Part of the World

Feixe should generally be dark enough for light to matter, but not so dark that navigation or puzzle readability suffers.

The baseline lighting concept is:

- low ambient illumination;
- subtle environmental fill;
- localized practical lights;
- laser beams as visually dominant light sources;
- restrained emissive surfaces;
- environment-based reflections where appropriate.

The lasers should feel physically present in the room rather than like colored lines drawn over the environment.

Activating a system may therefore alter not only the puzzle state but also the visual character of the space.

Darkness exists to give light importance, not to hide information from the player.

---

## 7. Progressive Unreality

The facility should not remain visually static throughout the game.

As puzzle complexity increases, the world should gradually become less comfortable and less easily explained.

### Early game

The facility appears rational.

Rooms have understandable proportions. Machinery appears organized. Architectural repetition suggests an institutional research environment. The player can still believe they understand the kind of place they entered.

### Middle game

Uncertainty increases.

Machines become larger or harder to interpret. Infrastructure appears increasingly excessive. Spaces may feel more isolated. Signs of previous activity become stranger. The relationship between the puzzle machinery and the facility becomes less obvious.

### Late game

The same visual language remains recognizable, but its composition becomes increasingly surreal.

Possible techniques include:

- unusual architectural proportions;
- excessive repetition;
- spaces that appear larger than the facility should physically contain;
- unexplained machinery;
- increasingly dominant optical phenomena;
- strange spatial relationships;
- stronger contrast and darkness;
- more aggressive sound and light interactions;
- subtle violations of established environmental expectations.

The progression should feel like the player is going **deeper into the same place**, not entering an unrelated fantasy world.

---

## 8. Psychedelia Through Escalation

Psychedelia should not define the starting aesthetic.

It should emerge gradually from the interaction between light, space, machinery, sound, and increasingly unusual environments.

The visual progression should parallel the soundtrack.

The early experience may be sparse, mechanical, atmospheric, and repetitive.

As the game becomes more complex, the soundtrack and environment can become more spatial, hypnotic, distorted, and psychedelic.

Bass, drums, synthesizers, machinery, and environmental ambience can together reinforce the sensation that the facility is becoming increasingly unstable or incomprehensible.

The final sections may become visually and sonically intense while still retaining recognizable elements of the original facility.

---

## 9. Unease Without Conventional Horror

Feixe is not intended to depend on monsters, gore, jumpscares, or conventional horror imagery.

Its discomfort should primarily come from implication.

The player should gradually move through questions such as:

> What is this machine?

> Why was this facility built?

> Why was it abandoned?

> Why am I being allowed to operate it?

> What happens when all of these systems are activated?

> Should I actually be doing this?

The facility may eventually reveal a disturbing purpose, but the visual language should not commit prematurely to a specific explanation.

Possible narrative interpretations — military technology, an experiment, a catastrophic event, spatial research, or something stranger — should remain possible until the lore is deliberately defined.

---

## 10. Human-Made Technology

Even when the technology becomes extraordinary, it should continue to look engineered.

Important visual cues include:

- fasteners;
- seams;
- access panels;
- brackets;
- rails;
- hinges;
- cables;
- connectors;
- mechanical pivots;
- protective housings;
- mounting points;
- labels;
- serial numbers;
- maintenance access.

Objects should suggest how they were assembled, installed, serviced, and operated.

This physical construction language helps prevent the environment from becoming generic abstract science fiction.

---

## 11. Architecture

Brutalism may influence the architecture, but it should not become the entire art direction.

Large concrete masses, repetitive institutional structures, exposed infrastructure, and severe geometry are useful because they support the feeling of a government research installation.

However, architecture should primarily serve the larger **retro-industrial laboratory** identity.

The environment should contrast:

**massive permanent architecture**

with

**precise optical machinery**

with

**small human-scale controls.**

This difference in scale should reinforce the impression that the player is interacting with only a tiny portion of something much larger.

---

## 12. Gameplay Readability Comes First

Atmosphere must never make the puzzle difficult to read for the wrong reasons.

Interactive devices should remain identifiable through consistent:

- silhouettes;
- proportions;
- interaction affordances;
- symbols;
- placement rules;
- animation;
- state feedback.

Environmental detail should support these devices rather than camouflage them.

The player may be uncertain about **what the facility means**, but should not be uncertain about **what they can interact with**.

Mystery belongs to the world and narrative.

Clarity belongs to the gameplay.

---

## 13. Intended Player Experience

The desired progression is approximately:

**curiosity → comprehension → mastery → suspicion → unease → fascination → revelation**

The player begins by learning how the machinery works.

Eventually, they become good at operating it.

Only then should the game increasingly question whether operating it was a good idea.

The final experience should leave the impression that every solved puzzle was also another step toward activating something whose true purpose was hidden from the player.

---

## 14. Moodboard Targets

Future moodboard research should explore references for:

- Soviet scientific laboratories from the 1960s–80s;
- Soviet control rooms and analog instrumentation;
- Eastern Bloc industrial design;
- retrofuturistic scientific equipment;
- brutalist research institutions;
- optical laboratories;
- particle-physics and high-energy research facilities;
- analog computers and control systems;
- industrial electrical infrastructure;
- institutional signage and technical typography;
- aged painted metal and laboratory materials;
- dark atmospheric science-fiction environments;
- environmental storytelling through abandoned technical spaces;
- psychedelic uses of light and geometry;
- spatially unsettling architecture.

References should be selected for **specific useful qualities**, not copied wholesale.

The goal is not to make "a Soviet-looking game."

The goal is to develop a coherent visual language for a mysterious scientific facility that feels historically grounded, technologically impossible, and progressively less understandable.

---

## 15. Current Direction vs. Open Questions

### Established direction

- Externally modeled GLB assets are allowed.
- The environment is a retro-industrial scientific facility with Soviet/Eastern Bloc influence.
- The world begins rational and becomes progressively stranger.
- Environmental storytelling is preferred over heavy exposition.
- The environment remains largely desaturated.
- Puzzle colors remain visually protected.
- Non-laser devices should generally remain chromatically neutral.
- Lasers are major contributors to the lighting composition.
- Technology should appear physical, engineered, and serviceable.
- Atmosphere must not compromise puzzle readability.
- The visual language should support an unknown and potentially disturbing facility purpose.

### Still open

- Exact architectural language.
- Exact decade and degree of historical authenticity.
- Final material palette.
- Surface wear and cleanliness level.
- Graphic-design and signage language.
- Device-family shape language.
- Symbol system for receivers and colors.
- Portal visual identity.
- Exact degree of environmental decay.
- How aggressively later levels distort the established visual language.
- Final lore and the true purpose of the facility.

These questions should be explored through moodboarding, visual studies, and the in-engine grey-box gallery before being frozen into the Design System.
